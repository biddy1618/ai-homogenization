\title{Did Generative AI Homogenize Online Writing?\\[4pt]
  \large A Multi-Corpus, Topic-Controlled Analysis (2009--2026)}
\author{Mark Nomellini}
\date{Draft v0.1 --- \today}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
We test the widely held intuition that generative AI (ChatGPT, publicly released in 2022Q4) has made
online writing more homogeneous. Using per-quarter diversity and similarity metrics on 30 dated text
corpora---26 Stack Exchange communities spanning high- and low-cognitive-load topics, plus Hacker News
comments, PubMed oncology abstracts, and arXiv computer-science and mathematics abstracts---and a layered battery of
controls (length control, four independent topic controls, a known-AI anchor, and
interrupted-time-series significance testing), we find that the dramatic surface-level convergence is a
length artifact; that a smaller but statistically real rise in contextual-embedding similarity survives
length control in most corpora; and that this residual rise is largely a shift in \emph{what} people
write about (topic composition), not a convergence of \emph{how} they write within a topic. We
therefore find no evidence of broad stylistic homogenization, but clear evidence of topic-level
homogenization: a narrowing of the topic mix and the emergence of a shared, AI-shaped set of themes. We
argue the topic-level effect is the more consequential one for epistemic diversity, and introduce a
live, open tracker to monitor it going forward.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

Since late 2022, a common complaint has surfaced: \emph{everything online is starting to sound the
same}. The em-dashes, the ``it's not just X---it's Y,'' the tidy three-part lists. The worry is that
as hundreds of millions of people route their writing through a handful of large language models, the
diversity of human expression---and, more importantly, the diversity of \emph{ideas}---quietly
collapses toward a shared machine mean.

This matters beyond aesthetics. Three stakes in particular:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Epistemic diversity.} If the range of answers to a question narrows, so does the pool
  of ideas a reader is exposed to. Homogenization of \emph{content} (not just style) is a narrowing of
  what gets said at all.
  \item \textbf{The integrity of the public record.} Scientific abstracts, legal opinions, and
  reference sites are the durable, searchable memory of a field. If that record converges toward a
  model's priors, we lose signal about what humans actually discovered or argued.
  \item \textbf{Search and discovery.} Search engines rank a top-$N$ of results. If those results
  increasingly repeat one another, the marginal information a searcher gains from reading more of them
  falls---the web gets wider but shallower.
\end{itemize}

There is also a prior reason to expect an effect. A meta-analysis of human--AI co-creation studies
finds a small but significant homogenization effect that persists after AI use ends
\citep{derooij2026}. And from behavioural economics, when people face more options or hold weaker
preferences they default to the status quo and to whatever is placed in front of them
\citep{samuelson1988}. An always-available, fluent default answer is exactly the kind of anchor that
theory predicts people will converge on.

\paragraph{The precise question.} ``Did AI homogenize writing?'' is too coarse to test. We decompose
it into three separable questions, and the paper's contribution is keeping them apart:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Aggregate similarity}---are any two texts from the same period more alike than they
  used to be?
  \item \textbf{Within-topic style}---holding the \emph{subject} fixed, do people write it more
  similarly (the ``everyone sounds like ChatGPT'' claim in its strict form)?
  \item \textbf{Topic composition}---is the \emph{distribution of subjects} narrowing, so texts look
  more alike simply because everyone now writes about the same things?
\end{enumerate}
A rise in (1) is compatible with (2), (3), or both. Most public commentary implicitly claims (2); we
find the evidence points mainly to (3).

\begin{framing}
Mark leans toward the affirmative ``AI is driving real, consequential homogenization.'' The data
supports that claim at the \emph{topic} level and rejects it at the \emph{style} level. The
introduction can honor the affirmative reading by foregrounding the topic-level finding and the
epistemic-diversity stakes, without overclaiming a style effect the data does not support.
\end{framing}

\section{Data}

All corpora are public, dated to the quarter, and run through 2026Q2, giving roughly 14 quarters of
post-ChatGPT observation against a long pre-2022 baseline. The ChatGPT marker throughout is 2022Q4.

\subsection{Stack Exchange (26 communities)}
From the June 2026 Stack Exchange data dump: 14 high-cognitive-load communities (e.g.\ Cross
Validated, Philosophy, Economics, Law, Computer Science, Physics) and 12 low-cognitive-load communities
(e.g.\ Seasoned Advice, Travel, Bicycles, Gardening, Role-playing Games, Board Games). The split tests
whether casual writing homogenizes more than expert writing. Five ``core'' communities (Cross
Validated, Philosophy, Economics, Seasoned Advice, Travel) carry every control; the other 21 carry the
aggregate and significance tests.

\subsection{External, non-Stack-Exchange corpora}
To test whether any finding is an artifact of Stack Exchange's terse, structured Q\&A style, we added
four corpora from entirely different platforms (Table~\ref{tab:externals}). arXiv mathematics serves as a
low-AI control: unlike computer science, its subject matter has little to do with AI itself. All four
carry the full pipeline, including topic control.

\section{Methodology}

The design is a layered battery of controls, each ruling out one benign explanation for an apparent
rise (Table~\ref{tab:layers}). Read top to bottom, each layer strips away a confound the previous one
left standing.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{The layered control design.}
\label{tab:layers}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lXX}
\toprule
Layer & What it measures & What it rules out \\
\midrule
Surface / lexical & type-token ratio, MTLD \citep{mccarthy2010}, Yule's K & vocabulary-diversity change \\
Length control & same metrics on first-100-token windows & answers simply got longer \\
LSA semantic & TF-IDF $\rightarrow$ SVD cosine, centroid variance & coarse topic drift \\
Contextual embedding & Sentence-BERT / MiniLM pairwise cosine \citep{reimers2019,wang2020minilm} & --- (the live signal) \\
Topic control (4 ways) & within-topic, same-question, decomposition & topic-mix shift vs.\ style convergence \\
Significance & segmented regression + Mann--Kendall \citep{mann1945} + bootstrap & noise; wrong break date \\
Known-AI anchor & distance from human answers to an actual ChatGPT answer & ``similar to each other'' $\neq$ ``similar to AI'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Two commitments deserve emphasis because they are where naive analyses most often go wrong.

\paragraph{Length control.} Embedding similarity rises mechanically with text length. We recompute
every headline metric on text truncated to the first 100 tokens. A signal that survives is not a
``posts got longer'' artifact.

\paragraph{Topic control, done four ways.} To separate \emph{style} from \emph{subject} we (a) cluster
with BERTopic \citep{grootendorst2022} and measure within-topic similarity over time; (b) apply a
Mann--Kendall trend test to the post-2022 within-topic series; (c) use the \emph{same-question} gold
standard, comparing only answers to the identical question---no clustering, no dropped data; and
(d) decompose the aggregate cosine into within- and between-topic components and track topic diversity
directly (global $k$-means, no dropped outliers). Agreement across four topic definitions is what makes
the topic-composition conclusion robust.

\paragraph{Anisotropy.} Raw embedding cosines are inflated because embeddings occupy a narrow cone
\citep{ethayarajh2019}. Where it matters we report the mean-centred (anisotropy-removed) variant, which
repeatedly flattens an apparent between-topic ``convergence.''

\section{Results}

\subsection{The dramatic surface rise is a length artifact}
Lexical-diversity metrics show a large apparent shift after 2022, but it tracks answer length
(Figure~\ref{fig:lexical}): raw TTR falls and raw TF-IDF similarity rises as answers grow longer, while
the length-controlled versions stay essentially flat. The effect never appears in already-verbose
corpora such as Philosophy. Answers got \emph{longer}, not more uniform.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{fig_lexical.png}
  \caption{Cross Validated, 2010--2026. Lexical diversity (MTLD, TTR), answer-to-answer TF-IDF
  similarity, mean answer length, and answer volume. Raw (dark) vs.\ length-controlled first-100-token
  (light) metrics: the raw TTR decline and similarity rise follow the growth in answer length and
  largely vanish under length control. Dashed line: ChatGPT release (2022Q4).}
  \label{fig:lexical}
\end{figure}

\subsection{A smaller contextual-embedding rise is real and survives length control}
The one signal that does not dissolve: Sentence-BERT pairwise similarity rises modestly from roughly
2023Q4 onward (Figure~\ref{fig:bert}), and the rise survives length control. By interrupted-time-series
slope change it is statistically significant in 18 of 26 Stack Exchange corpora (20 of 26 under length
control; Figure~\ref{fig:significance}), and it replicates in all four external corpora: Hacker News
(slope change $+0.00101$/quarter, $p=4.5\times10^{-9}$; Figure~\ref{fig:its}), PubMed ($+0.00080$,
$p=9\times10^{-4}$), arXiv-CS ($+0.00105$, $p=0.0014$), and arXiv mathematics ($+0.00130$,
$p=4\times10^{-10}$; length-controlled $p=0.007$).

The mathematics result is notable because it is a low-AI control: before 2022 its similarity was
\emph{falling} ($-0.00057$/quarter, $p\approx10^{-34}$), and after 2022Q4 that decline reverses into a
significant rise (post-break Mann--Kendall $p=0.0013$). The reversal begins only around 2024, and the
recent level is still below 2016.

Two honesty notes: the effect is broad but modest (significant partly because $N$ is large; for Cross
Validated the recent level is still below its own 2010--11 values), and PubMed and arXiv show a
pre-2022 upward trend---for them the change is an acceleration, not a clean break.

\subsection{The residual rise is largely topic composition, not within-topic style}
This is the central result. Once the subject is held fixed, the rise mostly disappears.

\paragraph{Stack Exchange.} Within-topic and same-question similarity is flat in 4 of 5 core corpora
(Figure~\ref{fig:sameq}). The decomposition locates the rise in \emph{between-topic} pairs while
within-topic similarity stays flat, and shows topic diversity shrinking and the topic mix drifting after
2022 (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}). Philosophy is the lone within-question exception.

\paragraph{External corpora.} Under BERTopic---the same method as the Stack Exchange headline---all
four externals show within-topic similarity flat while the aggregate rises (Figure~\ref{fig:withinhn}).
Hacker News: overall post-break Mann--Kendall $p=1.2\times10^{-4}$, within-topic $p=0.44$; PubMed:
overall $p=8\times10^{-4}$, within-topic $p=0.38$; arXiv-CS: overall $p=5\times10^{-4}$, within-topic
$p=1.0$; arXiv mathematics: overall $p=0.011$, within-topic $p=0.50$. In mathematics the decomposition
also shows the effective number of topics, which had been growing before 2022, turning to shrink after
it. A $k$-means decomposition initially flagged Hacker News as a within-topic exception
($p=0.004$), but that did not survive a second clustering method---within-topic tests are
method-sensitive. Every external also shows emergent LLM-era topics after 2022Q4
(e.g.\ \texttt{qa\_answers\_prompt}, \texttt{captioning\_mllms}, \texttt{segmentation\_radiomics\_dl}).

The reading: the rise is real, but it is mostly people converging on \emph{what} to write about, not
on \emph{how} to write it.

\subsection{No drift toward actual AI output; cognitive load does not explain the rise}
The most literal test---do human answers move toward an actual ChatGPT answer to the same
question?---is flat in 4 of 5 corpora, with Economics the lone and noisiest exception
(Figure~\ref{fig:anchor}). The rise is also independent of cognitive load: high- and low-load communities
track each other (Figure~\ref{fig:cogload}). The two lone exceptions (Philosophy in the same-question
test, Economics in the anchor test) fall on different sites and so read as noise, not corroboration.

\section{Real-World Consequences}

The finding is often heard as reassuring (``so writing \emph{didn't} homogenize''). We argue the
opposite is closer to the truth: the kind of homogenization we \emph{did} find---topic-level---is the
more consequential one.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Narrowing of the idea space, not just the word space.} A flat within-topic style but a
  shrinking, drifting topic mix means the change is in \emph{which questions get asked and answered at
  all}. Fewer effective topics is a direct measure of a narrower public conversation.
  \item \textbf{The scientific record inherits a model's priors.} Emergent post-2022 topic clusters in
  arXiv and PubMed show the research frontier reorganizing around AI-adjacent themes. Whether that is
  genuine progress or a bandwagon is exactly what a durable record should let us audit.
  \item \textbf{Search returns more pages that say less.} If top results increasingly share a topic
  profile, the marginal value of reading result \#5 after \#1--4 falls.
  \item \textbf{A feedback loop.} Models are trained on the web; the web is increasingly written with
  model help; topic-level convergence today biases the training data of tomorrow---a slow ratchet
  rather than a cliff.
  \item \textbf{Where it is \emph{not} alarming (yet).} We find no evidence that individual voice,
  within a subject, is collapsing, and no drift toward literal AI phrasing. The message is targeted, not
  apocalyptic: \emph{watch the topic mix, not the prose}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{framing}
How strong a claim should this section make? From most to least cautious: (a) ``a subtle but
structurally important narrowing worth monitoring''; (b) ``an early-warning sign of epistemic
concentration''; (c) ``evidence of a self-reinforcing homogenization loop.''
\end{framing}

\section{Monitoring Going Forward: A Live Search-Result Tracker}

The retrospective study measures the past. To measure the present, we built an open tracker that, every
week, collects Google's top results for a fixed panel of 45 informational questions across five
high-stakes domains (health, finance, law, science, technology), fetches each result's full page, and
scores how much the pages repeat one another with the Vendi score \citep{friedman2023vendi}---the
effective number of distinct pages---normalised by the number of pages analysed. Video results and
pages that cannot be fetched are excluded. The data and dashboard are public and versioned. The
series began in October 2026; with week-to-week movement still comparable to between-domain
differences, trends should be read only after several months of accumulation.

\section{Criticisms and Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Significance $\neq$ magnitude.} Large $N$ makes small slopes significant; several
  effects are small in absolute terms.
  \item \textbf{Timing $\neq$ causation.} The clearest bends lag ChatGPT by about a year and lack a
  clean 2022Q4 step in several corpora; PubMed and arXiv-CS were already trending up before 2022, and
  the arXiv-mathematics reversal only begins around 2024.
  \item \textbf{Topic-control fragility.} Within-topic tests depend on the topic definition: BERTopic
  discards 50--67\% of documents as outliers, and $k$-means and BERTopic disagreed on Hacker News. We
  rely on the agreement of four methods, not any one.
  \item \textbf{Anisotropy.} Raw between-topic ``convergence'' is largely embedding geometry; the
  mean-centred variant flattens it.
  \item \textbf{Single encoder.} All embedding results use one model family (MiniLM); a second encoder
  is pending.
  \item \textbf{Register bias.} Stack Exchange answers are atypically structured; the externals
  mitigate but do not eliminate this.
  \item \textbf{Lone exceptions.} Philosophy (same-question) and Economics (anchor) each show an effect
  on exactly one test, on different sites; reported, not suppressed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

The popular claim that generative AI made everyone write the same way is not supported by a layered,
multi-corpus, topic-controlled analysis. The large surface convergence is a length artifact; the
smaller, real, length-robust embedding rise is mostly a change in \emph{what} people write about, not
\emph{how}. What we do find is topic-level homogenization: a measurable narrowing and drift of the topic
mix, and the emergence of a shared, AI-shaped set of themes across forums, science, and technical
writing alike. That is a subtler but arguably more consequential effect for epistemic diversity than
stylistic sameness---and, because it is measurable, it is worth monitoring in the open. The honest
headline: \emph{not broad style homogenization, but a real and structurally important topical
convergence---worth watching, not yet worth alarm.}
